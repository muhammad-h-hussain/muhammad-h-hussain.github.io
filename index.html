About

I work in the Continuous Improvement team at the MHRA, the UK's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. My job is to help colleagues improve how work gets done: tightening processes, automating routine tasks, writing clear procedures and reporting on what is really happening.

I joined the MHRA in 2021 as a classifier in the Brexit transition and Variations teams, granting medicines licences at a time when the UK was rebuilding its regulatory processes after leaving the EU. That experience taught me how much careful coordination sits behind regulatory change, and how much good process design matters when deadlines are fixed in law.

Before the MHRA I worked across the public sector: as a Project Officer on Southwark Council's digital infrastructure programme, a Court Associate at HM Courts and Tribunals Service, and at NHS Blood and Transplant. Regulatory

PDN to PLPI conversions

Context
When the UK left the EU, Parallel Distribution Notices (PDNs) issued by the European Medicines Agency stopped being valid in Great Britain. Every parallel distributed medicine needed a UK parallel import licence (PLPI) to stay on the market.
Challenge
6,880 required licences, covering 272 products from 30 parallel distributors sourcing from 26 countries. Each one had to be converted accurately without disrupting supply to UK patients.
What I did
Owned the entire caseload single-handedly and ran it on Lean principles. Grouped cases by product family and distributor so similar work was processed together, and followed a standard work routine for every case, checking details against the original EU authorisation and the UK reference product. Built a visual management tracker showing each case's stage, with conditional formatting to flag urgent cases for escalation. Removed waiting waste by lining up quality checks in advance, so finished cases moved straight to grant instead of sitting idle.
Outcome
Took over the full list of 6,880 and worked through every outstanding case, ruling out those that were not proceeding because the holder did not opt in, the product lapsed or the conversion was withdrawn. More than 3,100 licences were granted in 2021, peaking at almost 1,500 in July alone, keeping parallel imported medicines available to UK patients.
Regulatory

QPPV and pharmacovigilance master file updates

Context
Every licence holder must name a QPPV (Qualified Person Responsible for Pharmacovigilance), who oversees the safety monitoring of its medicines. After Brexit, every UK licence needed UK pharmacovigilance details on record: a UK QPPV, or a UK-based national contact person supporting an EU-based QPPV, plus a UK pharmacovigilance system master file.
Challenge
1,363 licence records needed their QPPV and master file details updated accurately, so the safety monitoring of every product could be traced to the right UK contact.
What I did
Led a team of six and ran the work on the same Lean approach as the PDN conversions: cases grouped by licence holder, standard work for every update, and a visual management tracker with conditional formatting to flag urgent cases. Added a work in progress (WIP) limit so the team finished cases before starting new ones, which kept work flowing and stopped half-done updates building up. Flagged and resolved every exception, including licences with no QPPV on record and those already updated or cancelled.
Outcome
All 1,363 licences resolved, with 1,333 updated in under three months between April and July 2022 and the remaining exceptions closed out, so every licence carried correct UK pharmacovigilance details.
Process and technology

Robotic process automation performance analysis

Context
Software robots (robotic process automation, or RPA) screened incoming regulatory submissions before they reached human classifiers. After a software upgrade, the organisation needed a clear view of what the automation was really delivering, to inform decisions about its future.
Challenge
The raw logs recorded every processing step, so one submission could appear several times, and error descriptions were inconsistent. The post-upgrade figures also looked far better than expected, which needed explaining before anyone relied on them.
What I did
Alongside leading the 16-person team that processed RPA-handled submissions day to day, analysed 10,145 processing events across three reporting periods, counting each case once on its final outcome to give 5,200 unique submissions. Standardised the raw error text into consistent categories and built an auditable six-sheet report with live formulas. Traced the inflated post-upgrade figures to a defect that stopped processed cases being logged, so excluded that period and set a reliable baseline from the other two. Alongside the analysis, I worked with the supplier's engineers to fix a workflow fault that added about 6 seconds to every case, and helped build, test and put live an automated safeguard that stops processing and preserves case details if the robots fail.
Outcome
Established that the robots completed only 35.7% of cases without human input, with 42.0% correctly passed to manual handling as out of scope and 22.2% failing or flagged. This showed that the limited scope of the automation, rather than technical failure, was the biggest constraint on its value. The report gave senior leaders the evidence for their options analysis and led to the decision to end the platform's contract, removing a cost of £120,000 a year.
Process and technology

Classifier retimings mastersheet and target setting

Context
Each team's daily productivity targets come from timing data held in a retimings mastersheet. The Response team's targets had last been set in early 2025, before changes to systems and case mix.
Challenge
Targets had to reflect what the team could realistically achieve. Simple averages can be skewed by whoever logs the most cases, by bulk submissions and by time lost to system problems, so a fair and repeatable method was needed.
What I did
Rebuilt the mastersheet and designed the analysis: a stratified sample of 30 cases per worktype, weighted by each classifier's share of the log, with targets calculated on both mean and median so outliers were visible. Analysed 350 timed cases across four worktypes and six classifiers, and coded the free-text comments to separate system delays from the work itself.
Outcome
All four recommended targets were approved and now sit in the mastersheet. Two were confirmed, one was adjusted to reflect current system capability, and one rose from 53 to 74 cases a day (+40%) after processing time fell by 30 to 40%. The analysis pinpointed the main bottleneck: delays in internal case management systems affected 36% of cases in two worktypes, costing an estimated 25 to 45 minutes per classifier per day. I escalated this evidence to the system owner, and the resulting fix eliminated the delay completely, saving processing time for every user of the system.
Process and technology

Error reporting rebuild

Context
Errors found in licensing work are logged centrally and reported monthly to delivery managers, who use them in individual quality and performance discussions.
Challenge
Delivery managers found the monthly report hard to act on: it mixed months and did not show where each error came from. The underlying log also held duplicates and date errors that distorted individual error rates.
What I did
Rebuilt the log and the report using Lean Six Sigma thinking: defined the problem with the delivery managers, fixed the measurement first, then automated the output. Built a pre-send quality check and a duplicate case checker, moved reporting onto a validation-date basis so it reconciles with the three-month error rates used in performance discussions, added team-level error rates, and wrote the SOP for the new process.
Outcome
Removed over 100 duplicate entries from 1,638 records, so individual error rates rest on clean data before any performance conversation. Delivery managers now get a report that matches the figures they act on, governed by a published SOP.
Process and technology

SOP and policy work

Context
Standard operating procedures (SOPs) and training checklists underpin consistent, auditable work in a regulator.
Challenge
Training checklists and SOP reviews relied too heavily on individual practice, so procedures risked becoming inconsistent or out of date.
What I did
Designed a three-stage SOP review reminder and escalation process: reminders two months and two weeks before each review date, escalating to senior leaders, and a final notice once a document lapses, with every reminder filed for an audit trail. Consolidated licensing and validation SOPs into one controlled format, and authored a training best practice policy with a sign-off checklist for every trained process.
Outcome
Gave the department an auditable, repeatable way to keep its 26 SOPs in date ahead of an audit in January 2027, with owners, review dates and every reminder on record.
Project delivery

SharePoint classic to modern migration

Context
The department's documents sat on a shared classic SharePoint site that was being retired across the organisation. As part of an agency-wide data and information strategy, everything had to move to a new, dedicated modern site without disrupting day to day work.
Challenge
175,491 files and folders across four document libraries, nested up to 28 levels deep. Managers had to decide what to keep, and several teams' live Power BI reports and automated flows read directly from files on the old site, so a standard cutover would have broken them on day one.
What I did
Coordinated the migration end to end as the department's single point of contact, run through formal change control with weekly progress updates. Analysed the full export and turned it into 516 folder paths that managers could actually review, fixing a data flaw where the export mislabelled folders. Designed a mapping template with controlled dropdowns, colour coding and a safe default, so a missed reply could never cause data loss. Spotted the live reporting dependency two weeks before migration, escalated it to the head of the department, and designed and recommended a two-stage go-live, which was adopted, so connected files switched over only when their owners were ready.
Outcome
Went live on 27/07/2026 with all four libraries moved, permissions preserved and zero interruption to business-critical reporting. The final switchover followed seven days later, go-live access issues were fixed within two working days, and after a one-week extension requested by managers, every date held.
